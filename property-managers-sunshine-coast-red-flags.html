Key takeaway

Sunshine Coast landlords can spot a weak manager before signing by testing five areas: a written fee schedule that goes past the headline rate, evidence of the right Queensland licence or registration, a clear bond lodgement process within the 10 day RTA window, scheduled inspections with photo evidence, and suburb specific leasing experience. Queensland caps general tenancy bonds at 4 weeks rent and generally requires at least 12 months between rent increases, so a manager who cannot explain these rules is a risk.

Property Managers Sunshine Coast Red Flags Explained

A headline management percentage tells you very little. The pattern to watch is a low advertised rate paired with unclear add ons. Advertising, lease renewals, tribunal appearances and extra inspections can all shift the real cost of a tenancy, so a proposal that only quotes one figure deserves scrutiny.

Ask each candidate for a written schedule covering the ongoing management charge, the letting fee, inspection charges, renewal and advertising costs, and any additional services. Comparing two or three schedules side by side tends to reveal more than any single quote, because a slightly higher base rate with fewer add ons can work out cheaper across a full lease term.

If a manager hesitates to put fees in writing, treat that as the first red flag. the full guide covers the service scope and fee questions landlords should settle before signing an authority.

Licence and bond handling gaps

Some management work sits inside Queensland tenancy rules, so evasive answers here matter. People performing regulated real estate work in Queensland must hold the appropriate licence or registration. Ask directly who holds it, who supervises the work, and how compliance is checked. Vagueness on any of these points is a warning sign.

Bond handling is the most practical test. General tenancy bonds are capped at 4 weeks rent and must be lodged with the Residential Tenancies Authority within 10 days of receipt. A competent manager can explain how lodgement evidence is recorded and passed to you without prompting.

Push the same directness on rent increases. At least 12 months must generally pass between increases for general tenancies, periodic increases require at least 2 months written notice, and fixed term increases carry additional agreement and notice requirements. Because exemptions can apply, ask how the manager checks the current RTA position before any notice is issued. A manager with no defined process is guessing with your asset.

Weak inspection and reporting habits

Poor reporting is the red flag owners feel months after appointment, when rent entries, invoices and repair notes are hard to follow. Ask for sample statements before you sign, and check whether rent, invoices, inspection outcomes and repair notes are presented clearly enough to assess at a glance.

Inspections deserve the same treatment. Look for scheduled property condition reports with photos, tenant communication and maintenance follow up. Ask how often routine inspections occur, what photo evidence is included, and how unresolved maintenance is carried forward between visits. If a manager cannot show what an inspection record looks like, you have no way to verify property condition over time.

Year end documentation matters as well, particularly for owners building a portfolio. Inconsistent records across properties make tax time and condition tracking harder than they need to be.

No suburb specific leasing experience

Generic claims of local knowledge are easy to make and hard to verify. The better test is whether the manager can talk specifically about your property type and suburb.

  • Mooloolaba: beachside units, premium apartments, holiday letting demand and family homes near the beach raise distinct questions. Ask how comparable lettings are chosen and how long term residential management is separated from short stay assumptions.
  • Buderim: established hinterland streets, family homes, schools and Bruce Highway access suit a different tenant pool. Ask how screening weighs household fit and maintenance expectations for a family home rather than an apartment.
  • Nambour: hinterland rentals often need maintenance coordination where trades, tenant availability and owner approval must line up. Ask for recent leasing experience with similar stock.

A manager who cannot answer in these terms is offering promises, not local fit.

Vague answers on leasing process

The final cluster of warning signs sits in the day to day leasing workflow. Ask who reviews tenant applications, how references are handled, and at what point you approve a preferred applicant. Lease agreements should comply with Queensland law, and the first owner statement should arrive on a predictable cycle.

Equally telling is how a manager moves from rental appraisal to advertising and tenancy agreement preparation. Appraisals should draw on local vacancy data, comparable lettings and property condition, not a figure pulled from a suburb average.

Owners changing manager should request the fee schedule, inspection process and reporting examples before signing a new authority, and compare them against what the full guide sets out as standard practice. A candidate who resists these requests is telling you what the working relationship will feel like.

  1. Collect written fee schedules. Request the full schedule covering management, letting, inspection, renewal and additional fees from each manager under consideration.
  2. Verify compliance basics. Confirm the appropriate Queensland licence or registration, the bond lodgement process and how rent increase rules are checked.
  3. Request sample reporting. Ask to see sample statements, inspection photos and condition reports before signing a new authority.
  4. Match questions to the property. Frame questions around your suburb, property condition, likely tenant pool and service needs, then appoint.

Common questions

What is the biggest red flag when choosing a Sunshine Coast property manager? Reluctance to put fees in writing. A manager who will not provide a full schedule covering management, letting, inspection, renewal and additional charges leaves room for costs that a headline rate does not reveal.

How do I check a manager handles bonds correctly in Queensland? General tenancy bonds are capped at 4 weeks rent and must be lodged with the Residential Tenancies Authority within 10 days of receipt. Ask how lodgement evidence is recorded and passed to you, and be cautious of any manager without a clear answer.

Should local experience change which manager I pick? Yes. A beachside unit in Mooloolaba, a family home in Buderim and a hinterland rental in Nambour each involve different tenant demand, maintenance access and inspection logistics, so ask for experience with your specific property type and suburb.
